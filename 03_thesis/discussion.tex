\chapter{Discussion}
\label{discussion}

In this thesis I first reproduced two behavioral models (BFE- and GFE agent) from \textcite{schwoebel2018,delaar2023} that are capable of perception, planning, and action. Both are based on free energy functionals from which message passing schemes are derived. Message passing enables them with inference, which in turn gives rise to their full behavioral repertoire. The GFE agent is based on the BFE agent but additionally entails a mechanism that reduces the uncertainty of its hidden state (part of its generative model) by seeking information in the environment via actions, i.e., it is curious.
In a second step, I expanded the free energy functional of the BFE agent to introduce a hidden context state in the generative model, which gave rise to the Context-BFE agent. The corresponding message passing algorithm was derived from this expanded free energy. In other words, I translated the Bayesian contextual control model from \textcite{schwoebel2021} to a Forney factor graph representation with corresponding Lagrangian.
In a third step, I took the integration of the information seeking mechanism in the GFE agent as an example and followed the formal derivations from \textcite{delaar2025} to introduce this mechanism to the Context-BFE agent for the context state, i.e., the mechanism leads to information seeking that reduces uncertainty of the hidden context state. This integration finally led to the Context-GFE agent. From its customized free energy functional I derived the corresponding message passing algorithm.
I tested all four agents in the simulated T-maze environment which is frequently used to reveal information seeking behavior. There I showed that the newly developed Context-GFE agent shows similar behavior to the GFE agent, which strongly suggests that the integration of the information seeking mechanism in the Context-GFE agent was successful, i.e., it is curious like the GFE agent and is driven to reduce uncertainty over its hidden (context) state. As a baseline, I also gave the results for both non-curious agents (BFE and Context-BFE), which both show similar (non-information seeking) behavior.
Beyond that, I generally addressed the kind of behavior that results from the information seeking mechanism in both curious agents (not uniquely tied to the newly developed Context-GFE agent).

\section{Interpretation}

The primary goal of this work was to develop an agent that performs information seeking over its context state. The evaluation of this attempt then consisted of testing the agent (Context-GFE) in the T-maze environment, which is a typical simulation environment to show this kind of behavior, and comparing it with the GFE agent that also performs information seeking. Both the Context-GFE- and GFE agent show similar behavior that is also largely different to the two non-curious agents (BFE and Context-BFE). This circumstance hints that the integration of the information seeking mechanism in the Context-GFE agent was successful because it behaves akin to the GFE agent. This requires further explanation: The GFE agent is driven to reduce uncertainty regarding its (normal) hidden state while the Context-GFE agent does the same over its additional context state (albeit also over its normal hidden state). The question arises why both agents then show similar behavior if they have the information seeking mechanism for different variables in its generative models. The answer to this question lies in the specific parts of the T-maze that is modeled with these variables. The GFE agent's hidden state \(s\) models the location in the maze (Field) and the type of the maze (MazeType). The Context-GFE agent's hidden state \(s\) only contains the location while the maze type is outsourced to the context state \(c\). Importantly, the location in the maze always gets immediately disclosed at each observation, i.e., both agents never have any uncertainty regarding their location in the maze. The only ambiguity they have is over the maze type. While this property of the T-maze is assigned to different variables in both agents (\(s\) and \(c\)) they also perform information seeking over the respective variable that contains the maze type. In other words, both agents perform information seeking over different variables but the property of the environment (maze type) that is modeled via both variables is the same. Concluding, both curious agents attempt to reduce their uncertainty regarding the maze type and the resulting behavior naturally becomes similar. There is a peculiarity that needs to be addressed: The Context-GFE agent also performs information seeking over its (normal) hidden state \(s\). In the simulations this has no effect because there never is uncertainty regarding \(s\). This is because \(s\) in the Context-GFE agent only models the location in the maze, which is always known to the agent as outlined before.

Next, the question of why it is necessary to simulate the experiments with different \(r\)- and \(\alpha\) values to show the curiosity drive of the Context-GFE agent may arise. This becomes obvious when viewing curiosity as a drive that has a "hard" currency, which is information. At each timestep during the simulations when the agent must do an action, the value of this action is always evaluated with respect to two things: First, the amount of information that the agent expects that following observations reveal (epistemic value in \cite{friston2015}), and second the likelihood of receiving preferred observations with respect to its goal prior (pragmatic value in \cite{friston2015}). Specifically, both are always in competition with each other and importantly are weighed against using the same quantity, which is information/surprise measured in log probabilities. Following these competing drives it then becomes natural to modulate both drives to make their varying influences visible. This is achieved by modulating the parameters \(\alpha\) and \(r\). More extreme \(\alpha\) values yield more information at both arm fields because the resulting observation there reveals the maze type with more certainty. In this situation the curious agent is more inclined to directly move to the arms instead of going to the cue field, i.e., the probabilities of directly moving the cue become smaller as a result. Higher \(r\) values increase the pragmatic values of the arm fields and the agent is thus more inclined to directly move to the arms, i.e., it is less likely to go to the cue (which has lower pragmatic value).

Furthermore, it might also be controversial why \(\alpha\) was modulated and not an alternative parameter that controls the relation between the maze type and the cue. In principle, the choice between the two options is not too important. Recall that the parameter \(\alpha\) controls the relation between maze type and cue location. Effectively, the agent has two options to reduce its uncertainty regarding the maze type: First, via the arms where it either observes the reward or not, and second via the cue. When modulating \(\alpha\) the usefulness of the first option varies and when modulating the prescribed alternative parameter the usefulness of the second option varies. In conclusion, with respect to the information seeking drive the specific balance between the usefulness of both options decide whether the agent rather moves to the arms or moves to the cue to learn about the maze type. This balance can equivalently be modulated by \(\alpha\) or by the alternative parameter, which renders both options qualitatively similar regarding epistemic value. However, there is something else to consider here: When the agent chooses the first option and thus moves to the arms the corresponding (epistemic) value of information competes with the (pragmatic) value of realizing preferred (or avoiding disliked) observations, which adds extra noise to the results, i.e., the agent's inclination of initially going to the cue is not only based on the balance between both options but is additionally influenced by the pragmatic value in the arms. With respect to this situation modulating the prescribed alternative parameter could be a better option because it more directly relates to the agent's inclination of initially moving to the cue (the alternative parameter relates the maze type with the cue). The reason why I instead opted for the parameter \(\alpha\) instead of the alternative parameter is the work's comparability with other works where also \(\alpha\) is modulated (e.g., in \cite{friston2015,delaar2025}).

Finally, the results of experiment 3 "Long-Term Success" reveal worse performance for both curious agents compared to the non-curious agents in their ability of finding the reward. I presume this is due to an additional attention parameter that usually gets inferred by active inference agents (e.g. \(\gamma\) in \cite{friston2015}), which was not taken into account in this work. The attention parameter modulates the probabilities in the action belief distributions. In this work the parameter is implicitly \(\gamma = 1\). \textcite{delaar2023} have shown that curious agents initially move far more likely to the cue when \(\gamma = 10\) (leads to larger probabilities in \cref{fig:21}), i.e., instead of gambling for the reward by directly moving to the arms they first seek information far more regularly with increased attention and thus are expected to find the reward more often. The integration and inference of the attention parameter \(\gamma\) in the agents' factor graphs and corresponding message passing schemes remains an open problem.


\section{Limitations}

While the results of both curious agents are qualitatively similar, they are not equal, meaning that although the Context-GFE agent also performs information seeking like the GFE agent, there are subtle differences that are addressed in the following: The Context-GFE agent shows a more abrupt transition of probabilities from smaller \(r\) values and \(\alpha\) values near \(0.5\) to larger \(r\) values and more extreme \(\alpha\) values in experiment 1. This might be due to the following situation: The Context-GFE performs information seeking over the context state, which introduced cyclic dependencies in its factor graph. Furthermore, multiple GFE-based messages (from future likelihood nodes) converge on these cycles. GFE-based messages introduce mean-field approximations at the respective likelihood nodes, which is known to have the effect forming local belief distributions that are overconfident (\cite[Chapter~10]{bishop2006}), i.e, uncertainties are not fully respected. The abrupt change of probabilities described before then might be a symptom of this overconfidence, where behavior changes abruptly because uncertainties, which would usually lead to a gradual shift, are not fully respected. In principle, also the GFE agent contains GFE-based messages with mean-field approximations. This suggests that the cycles, which only exist for the Context-GFE agent, lead to inference results where overconfidences that are local to the likelihood distributions propagate to the action belief distributions (whose probabilities of "going to the cue" are given in the results of Experiment 1).
While the (subtle) differences between the newly developed Context-GFE agent and the GFE agent are evident the underlying reasons remain obscure and require further investigation.


\section{Outlook}

Having developed both the Context-BFE- and Context-GFE agent, they can now be used in Bayesian model comparison. As outlined earlier, the agents can equivalently be viewed as models of behavior. This might be achieved as follows: First, empirical data (e.g., from clinical studies) consisting of time series of observations and actions can be integrated into the factor graphs of both models (agents) by introducing delta distributions at the respective observation- and action related edges that assign all probability mass at the respective data points in the time series. Second, likelihood distribution, transition distribution, state prior, action prior, goal prior, and context prior get initialized with assumed parameters for both models. Third, the message passing algorithms of both models are executed, i.e., the belief distributions are inferred. Fourth, the variational free energy \(F\) of both models is evaluated (with respect to their generative model and inferred belief distributions).
Finally, the model that has a lower free energy value is assumed to have greater evidence as a model that could have generated the empirical data. For example, when the Context-GFE model yields a lower variational free energy compared to the Context-BFE model, then it is more likely that the true underlying mechanism that generated the empirical data (e.g., brain function of the patients) performed contextual information seeking. Crucially, other models of behavior might be able to explain the given empirical data even better, i.e., it is not definitely proven that the time series result from the contextual information seeking mechanism in the case the Context-GFE model yields a lower \(F\) value compared to the Context-BFE model.
